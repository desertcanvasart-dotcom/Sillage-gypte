# Destination Page Methodology

*A repeatable system for building each destination page — Cairo, Aswan, Alexandria, and the rest — at the same quality bar as Luxor.*

The goal is consistency without sameness. Every page should feel like it came from the same hand, but no two should feel stamped from the same mould. This document is the hand.

---

## Before you write: the four decisions

Do not open the page template until these four are settled. They determine which sections exist and how the page is built. Settling them first is what prevents the mid-build drift that wastes a session.

**1. The single true thing.** What is the one idea this destination is *actually* about — not its monument list, but its essence? Luxor's is *density* (so much standing in so small a place). Cairo's might be *collision* (twenty million people living on top of four thousand years). Aswan's might be *the frontier* (where Egypt ends and Africa begins; where the Nile is still beautiful). Write this sentence down before anything else. Every other choice on the page serves it. If you cannot name it, you are not ready to write.

**2. The signature module.** Luxor earned a custom layout — "The Two Banks" — because its geography *is* a structure. Not every city earns one, and a forced signature is worse than none. Decide honestly: does this destination have an organising principle that wants to become layout? (See the *Signature modules* section below.) If yes, design it. If no, the page uses the standard essential-sites list and spends its boldness on photography instead. Either is fine. A page with no gimmick and excellent restraint beats a page with a strained one.

**3. The honest caveat.** Every destination has something a luxury client should be warned about before they arrive, and saying it is the brand. Luxor's is the heat and the crowds. Cairo's is the chaos, the traffic, the hustle around the pyramids. Aswan's is that it is *slow* — wonderful if you want that, frustrating if you expected Luxor's pace. Name the caveat now; it will shape the "When to visit," the guide quote, and the FAQ. A destination page with no honest caveat reads like a brochure.

**4. The access hook.** What can *you* arrange here that the package-tour client cannot get? Luxor's is Nefertari and tombs beyond the standard ticket. Cairo's might be the Grand Egyptian Museum before opening, or a private hour at a normally-crowded site. Aswan's might be a specific island, a felucca captain, a Nubian house. This is the single most important commercial element on the page — it is the reason the price is justified. If you cannot name a real one, the destination may not belong in the portfolio at this tier.

## The section inventory

These are the building blocks. **Core** sections appear on every page. **Conditional** sections appear only when the destination warrants them. Order is fixed; presence is not.

### Core — every page has these

| Section | Job | The rule that keeps it good |
|---|---|---|
| **Hero** | Name + one essence line | The essence line is the *lede from your copy*, never a tagline cliché. Ban "open-air museum," "jewel of the Nile," "timeless," "hidden gem," "city of a thousand minarets." If you've read the phrase on a competitor's page, it's dead. |
| **At-a-glance ledger** | Four facts, scannable | Best time · Ideal length · Style · Pairs well with. Always exactly these four. Same order every page — it becomes a learnable pattern across the site. |
| **Overview** | The essence, in two paragraphs | Para one: what the place *was* and is. Para two: what makes it extraordinary *and how you handle it differently*. The second paragraph must contain operator judgment, not description. |
| **Essential sites** | The monuments that matter | Curate, do not catalogue. 4–7 sites maximum. Each gets a one-paragraph entry with a *when/how* note (best light, best hour, why most people get it wrong). A site with nothing operator-specific to say about it does not earn a slot. |
| **When to visit** | Three seasons, honestly | Always three columns: the classic season, the sweet spot (highlighted — this is your recommendation), the hard season (named honestly, with the compensating reward). The honest caveat from decision 3 lives here. |
| **Stay & travel** | Where to sleep, how to move | Two columns. *Where to stay*: 2 named properties with one honest line each — setting over stars. *Getting there & around*: how they arrive (and the elevated alternative — dahabiya, not felucca, for the Nile). |
| **Guide quote** | Expertise, shown not claimed | One Egyptologist, one paragraph, making an argument the client couldn't make themselves. The quote should *reframe* the destination, not praise it. |
| **Gallery** | The light does the talking | 6 frames, each captioned with subject + the moment (light/hour). On a luxury page the photography carries more weight than the prose; the captions promise specific light. |
| **FAQ** | The real questions, answered straight | 5–6 questions. At least one must address the honest caveat. At least one must surface the access hook. Answers are short and never defensive. |
| **Related journeys** | The commercial bridge | 3 journey cards that actually include this destination. This is where the page converts from inspiration to enquiry. |
| **Final CTA** | "See [City], properly." | The closing line is always *"See [City], properly."* — it's becoming the brand's signature sign-off. Keep it. |

### Conditional — only when earned

| Section | Use it when… | Skip it when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Signature module** (custom layout) | the destination has an organising principle that wants to be structure (Luxor's two banks; Aswan's islands; Cairo's layers of time) | the city is best served by a clean essential-sites list |
| **The water** (felucca/dahabiya feature) | the Nile or a sail is central to the experience (Aswan, the Nile itself) | the destination is land-locked in feel (Cairo's relationship to the river is incidental to the visit) |
| **A second voice** | the destination has a distinct human story a non-Egyptologist tells better — a Nubian host in Aswan, a Coptic scholar in Old Cairo | one expert voice covers it |

## Signature modules: the test

A signature module is the one thing a page is remembered by. It is also the easiest thing to get wrong. Apply this test before building one:

> Does the destination's **real-world structure** map onto a **layout structure** that teaches the reader something true?

- **Luxor → The Two Banks.** Geography is the lesson: living east, dead west, the Nile between. The split-screen layout *is* the geography. Passes.
- **Aswan → The Islands.** Aswan is read island by island — Elephantine, Kitchener's, Philae. A module that moves between islands could work. Possible, if the islands genuinely carry the structure. Test it honestly.
- **Cairo → The Layers.** Pharaonic, Coptic, Islamic, modern — Cairo is a vertical stack of eras on one ground. A "layers of time" module (descending through the city's ages) could be its signature. Strong candidate.
- **Counter-example.** Do *not* invent "The Three Temples" for a city just because it has three temples. Three is a number, not a structure. The module must encode a *truth about how the place works*, or it's decoration — and decoration is the thing the brand doesn't do.

When in doubt: no module. Restraint is on-brand. A strained signature is not.

## The voice rules (non-negotiable, every page)

These are the brand. They do not flex per city.

- **Short declarative sentences.** British English. The register of a well-travelled, honest friend who happens to be an expert — not a marketer.
- **No rhetorical questions.** Ever.
- **No superlatives you can't defend.** "The largest religious complex of the ancient world" is defensible. "The most magical place on earth" is not. If a fact-checker could challenge it, soften it or cut it.
- **The honest-operator move, at least once per page.** Tell the client something true that costs you a little — Tutankhamun's tomb is nearly bare; the summer is brutal; this site is overrun by eleven. Honesty is the luxury signal. It says: *we are advising you, not selling to you.*
- **Operator judgment over description.** Anyone can describe Karnak. Only you can say *enter it in late afternoon when the coaches have gone.* Every site entry needs at least one sentence only an operator could write.
- **Specificity over adjectives.** "134 columns the size of mature trees" beats "vast and awe-inspiring." Always reach for the number, the hour, the concrete detail.

## The factual-verification step (do not skip)

Each destination carries traps. Before a page ships, verify — do not trust memory or first draft:

- **Tier-appropriate vocabulary.** *Dahabiya*, not felucca, for multi-night sailing at this tier. Watch for the equivalent trap in every city — the word that quietly signals the wrong price bracket.
- **Numbers and dates.** Opening years, site counts, distances, recent changes (museum openings, site closures, route changes). Egypt's monument landscape changes — the GEM opening, the Sphinx Avenue reopening, mummy relocations. A wrong date in front of a knowledgeable client costs trust the whole page was built to earn.
- **Access claims.** Only promise access you can actually arrange. "Special access where available" is honest; naming a guaranteed private opening you can't deliver is a refund and a bad review.
- **Current status.** Verify anything that could have changed: which tombs are open, which tickets exist, what a site is now called. When unsure, write the page and flag the line for a live check before publish — don't ship the guess.

> Build a short per-city *facts ledger* as you go (like the one already locked for Dendera). It's faster the second time and it's what keeps the trilingual EN/ES/JA versions consistent.

## Design module logic

The page is assembled from the modules in the Luxor build. Reuse the locked tokens and the section components; vary only what the content demands.

- **Palette, type, spacing: locked.** Do not re-decide them per city. The Nile-dusk green, papyrus, gold; Marcellus / EB Garamond / Jost. Consistency here is what makes ten pages feel like one brand.
- **What varies per city:** the hero gradient's centre of warmth, the gallery's placeholder tones (until real photography lands), and the presence/absence of the signature module. That's it.
- **The dark-heavy palette raises the photo bar.** On dark sections, only excellent photography survives. If a city's image library is weak, lean the page lighter (more papyrus sections) until the photography is ready — don't ship a dark page with thin images.
- **Sticky journey bar: always on.** It's the conversion spine. Same component every page.

## The build order (one session per city)

A clean sequence that resists the drift toward polish-before-substance:

1. **Decide the four** (essence, signature, caveat, access hook). Write them at the top of the working file. ~20 minutes. Do not skip to design.
2. **Draft the copy** — all sections, in the voice, in markdown. Content before pixels, always.
3. **Run the factual-verification step.** Fix and build the city facts ledger.
4. **Choose modules** — core set + any conditional, + signature if it passed the test.
5. **Assemble the page** from locked components. This is mechanical if 1–4 are done.
6. **The Chanel pass** — read it back and remove one thing. The strained line, the second adjective, the module that didn't quite earn its place. The page should feel like it was *edited*, not just written.
7. **Trilingual pass** — EN → ES/JA, re-authored not translated, per your established method. The facts ledger keeps all three consistent.

---

## The one-line quality test

Before any destination page ships, it must pass this:

> **Could a knowledgeable client tell this page was written by someone who has actually run tours here — and would they trust the advice enough to hand over their holiday?**

If yes, ship it. If it reads like it could have been assembled from a guidebook, it isn't ready, regardless of how good it looks.
