## Overview

For five hundred years Luxor was Thebes, the religious and political heart of Egypt at the height of its power. The pharaohs of the New Kingdom raised their great temples to Amun on the east bank, where the sun rose, and cut their tombs into the hills of the west bank, where it set. Nowhere else is so much of ancient Egypt still standing, still painted, still legible.

What makes Luxor extraordinary is not any single monument but the sheer density of them — and the fact that, with the right access and the right hours, you can still have much of it to yourself. We time everything around the light and the crowds: the Valley of the Kings at opening, Karnak in the gold of late afternoon, and a painted tomb that most visitors never enter.

## The essential sites

### The Valley of the Kings — *West Bank*
Sixty-three royal tombs cut into the Theban hills, their corridors painted with the funerary texts meant to carry the pharaoh safely into the afterlife. We include tombs beyond the standard ticket and, when it is open, the tomb of Seti I — the finest of them all.

### Karnak Temple — *East Bank*
The largest religious complex ever built, raised and added to over two thousand years. Its Hypostyle Hall — 134 columns the size of mature trees — is best entered in the low light of late afternoon, when the carvings deepen and the coaches have gone.

### Luxor Temple — *East Bank*
The temple at the heart of the modern town, once joined to Karnak by a two-mile avenue of sphinxes. It is at its most beautiful after dark, lit gold against the night.

### Deir el-Bahari — Temple of Hatshepsut — *West Bank*
The mortuary temple of Egypt's most successful female pharaoh, rising in three colonnaded terraces against a sheer cliff — architecture that looks three thousand years ahead of its time.

### Deir el-Medina — *West Bank*
The village of the artisans who cut and painted the royal tombs, and the small, jewel-like tombs they made for themselves — the most personal and human art anywhere in Egypt.

### The Ramesseum & Medinet Habu — *West Bank*
The mortuary temples of Ramesses II and Ramesses III. The latter is among the best-preserved and least-visited major temples in the country, its colour still vivid on the walls.

## When to visit

### October – April — *The classic season*
Warm, clear days and cool evenings — the most comfortable time to stand before a temple at midday. It is also the busiest, which is precisely why our pre-opening and private-evening access matters most now.

### Late October & early April — *The sweet spot*
The weather has turned but the high-season crowds have not fully arrived. Long golden light, and the sites breathe.

### May – September — *The quiet heat*
Hot, often above 40°C, but dramatic and nearly empty. For travellers willing to start at dawn and rest through the afternoon, the rewards — and the prices — are considerable.

## How long to stay

Two days covers the headline sites without rushing. Three to four days lets you reach the quieter west-bank tombs and temples and gives Luxor the depth it deserves. Most of our travellers pair Luxor with a private Nile journey to or from Aswan.

## Getting there & around

**Getting there:** Luxor International Airport (LXR) receives direct flights from Cairo — about 80 minutes — and seasonal international routes from Europe. Many of our travellers instead arrive or depart by private felucca, sailing the Nile between Luxor and Aswan.

**Getting around:** The sites straddle the river: the temples of the living on the east bank, the necropolis on the west. We move you privately by car and river crossing, timed around the heat and the crowds rather than a fixed schedule.

## Luxor, answered (FAQ)

**How many days should I spend in Luxor?**
Two days covers the essential sites without rushing; three to four days allows the quieter west-bank tombs and temples. Many travellers combine Luxor with a private Nile journey to or from Aswan.

**What is the best time of year to visit Luxor?**
October to April is the classic season — warm days and cool evenings. Late October and early April offer the best balance of good weather and fewer crowds. Summer is hot but dramatic and nearly empty.

**Can you visit the Valley of the Kings privately?**
The valley is a public site, but we time your visit to opening, include tombs beyond the standard three-tomb ticket, and arrange special access where it is available — so it never feels like a queue.

**Is Luxor or Cairo better for ancient sites?**
They are different, not ranked. Cairo has the pyramids and the Grand Egyptian Museum; Luxor has the greatest concentration of standing temples and painted tombs anywhere on earth. The ideal journey includes both.

**How do you get from Cairo to Luxor?**
A direct flight of about 80 minutes, which we arrange, or as part of a private Nile journey. All transfers are handled privately, door to door.
