About Luxor

Five hundred years at the centre of the world

For five hundred years Luxor was Thebes, the religious and political heart of Egypt at the height of its power. The pharaohs of the New Kingdom raised their great temples to Amun on the east bank, where the sun rose, and cut their tombs into the hills of the west bank, where it set. Nowhere else is so much of ancient Egypt still standing, still painted, still legible.

What makes Luxor extraordinary is not any single monument but the sheer density of them — and the fact that, with the right access and the right hours, you can still have much of it to yourself. We time everything around the light and the crowds: the Valley of the Kings at opening, Karnak in the gold of late afternoon, and a painted tomb that most visitors never enter.

The essential sites

The Two Banks

The Nile divides Luxor as it divided Theban life: the temples of the living to the east, where the sun rises — the city of the dead to the west, where it sets.

Where the sun rises

East Bank

The temples of the living

Karnak Temple

The largest religious complex of the ancient world, raised and added to over two thousand years. Its Hypostyle Hall — 134 columns the size of mature trees — is best entered in the low light of late afternoon, when the carvings deepen and the coaches have gone.

Luxor Temple

The temple at the heart of the modern town, once joined to Karnak by a nearly two-mile avenue of sphinxes. It is at its most beautiful after dark, lit gold against the night.

Where the sun sets

West Bank

The necropolis

The Valley of the Kings

Sixty-three royal tombs cut into the Theban hills, their corridors painted with the texts meant to carry the pharaoh safely into the afterlife. We include tombs beyond the standard ticket — among them, when it is open, Seti I, the finest of them all. Tutankhamun's tomb is small and nearly bare; we will tell you honestly whether it belongs in your morning.

The Tomb of Nefertari Valley of the Queens

The most beautiful painted chamber in Egypt, and its most exclusive ticket. Visits are short and strictly limited — which is exactly why we arrange them.

Deir el-Bahari — Temple of Hatshepsut

The mortuary temple of Egypt's most successful female pharaoh, rising in three colonnaded terraces against a sheer cliff — architecture that looks three thousand years ahead of its time.

Deir el-Medina

The village of the artisans who cut and painted the royal tombs, and the small, jewel-like tombs they made for themselves — the most personal and human art anywhere in Egypt.

The Ramesseum & Medinet Habu

The mortuary temples of Ramesses II and Ramesses III. The latter is among the best-preserved and least-visited major temples in the country, its colour still vivid on the walls.

When to visit

Reading the season

October – April

The classic season

Warm, clear days and cool evenings — the most comfortable time to stand before a temple at midday. It is also the busiest, which is precisely why our pre-opening and private-evening access matters most now.

Late October & early April

The sweet spot

The weather has turned but the high-season crowds have not fully arrived. Long golden light, and the sites breathe.

Our recommendation
May – September

The quiet heat

Hot, often above 40°C, but dramatic and nearly empty. For travellers willing to start at dawn and rest through the afternoon, the rewards — and the prices — are considerable.

Getting there & around

Arriving as you should

Luxor receives direct flights from Cairo — about 80 minutes — and seasonal routes from Europe. Many of our travellers instead arrive or depart by private dahabiya, sailing the Nile between Luxor and Aswan under canvas, as the first travellers did.

The sites straddle the river: the temples of the living on the east bank, the necropolis on the west. We move you privately by car and river crossing, timed around the heat and the crowds rather than a fixed schedule. Two days covers the headline sites without rushing; three to four gives Luxor the depth it deserves.

Most people are shown the Valley of the Kings in an hour and moved on. Give it a morning, with someone who can actually read the walls, and a tomb stops being a corridor of pictures — it becomes the most ambitious thing a civilisation ever attempted.

Questions

Luxor, answered

How many days should I spend in Luxor?
Two days covers the essential sites without rushing; three to four days allows the quieter west-bank tombs and temples. Many travellers combine Luxor with a private dahabiya journey to or from Aswan.
What is the best time of year to visit Luxor?
October to April is the classic season — warm days and cool evenings. Late October and early April offer the best balance of good weather and fewer crowds. Summer is hot but dramatic and nearly empty.
Can you visit the Valley of the Kings privately?
The valley is a public site, but we time your visit to opening, include tombs beyond the standard three-tomb ticket, and arrange special access where it is available — so it never feels like a queue.
Is the tomb of Nefertari worth the separate ticket?
Yes, without hesitation. Visits are limited to ten minutes, and they are the finest ten minutes in Egypt. We arrange the ticket and time it within your west-bank morning.
Is Luxor or Cairo better for ancient sites?
They are different, not ranked. Cairo has the pyramids and the Grand Egyptian Museum; Luxor has the greatest concentration of standing temples and painted tombs anywhere on earth. The ideal journey includes both.
How do you get from Cairo to Luxor?
A direct flight of about 80 minutes, which we arrange, or as part of a private Nile journey. All transfers are handled privately, door to door.
