# Journey Page Methodology

*A repeatable system for building each journey page — The Private Nile, Ancient Cairo & Luxor, The Complete Egypt, and the rest — at the same quality bar.*

A destination page sells a *place*. A journey page sells a *story through places* — and it is the page that actually converts an admirer into an enquiry. It carries more commercial weight than any other page on the site, so the bar is higher and the honesty matters more: a mismatched journey is the single biggest source of a disappointed luxury client. This document encodes the judgment that keeps every journey page consistent, honest, and persuasive.

> Read alongside the **Destination Page Methodology**. The voice rules, the locked design tokens, and the factual-verification discipline are shared — they are not repeated in full here. This document covers what is *different* about a journey.

---

## Before you write: the four decisions

Settle these before opening the template. They decide the page's shape and its signature.

**1. The spine.** Every journey has a structural truth — the thing that explains *how it moves*. The Private Nile's spine is *downstream* (the Nile flows north, so Aswan→Luxor runs with the current). The Complete Egypt's spine might be *north to deep south* (Cairo to Abu Simbel, civilisation thinning as you go). A city-pair journey's spine might be *contrast* (the chaos of Cairo against the stillness of the river). Name the spine first — it becomes the signature module (the day-by-day layout) and the logic the whole page hangs on. A journey with no spine is just a list of days.

**2. Who it's for — and who it isn't.** This is the journey-page equivalent of the destination's honest caveat, and it is more important here because the commitment is larger. The Private Nile is for someone who wants seven *slow* days on the water — and explicitly not for someone who wants to tick every site at speed. Name both sides now. The "who it isn't for" sentence is the most trust-building line on the page; it tells the buyer you're advising, not selling. Write it before the copy.

**3. The access hooks, sequenced.** A destination page names access in general. A journey page must show *where in the itinerary each hook lands* — Kom Ombo at dusk on Day 3, Edfu at opening on Day 4, the Valley of the Kings before the coaches on Day 6. The hooks are the proof that the price buys something a convoy can't. Map them to specific days before drafting, so the day-by-day has a reason to exist beyond logistics.

**4. The emotional arc.** A journey has a shape of feeling, not just geography. Where does it begin (anticipation, arrival), where does it peak (the one unforgettable moment), how does it close (a quiet end, or a crescendo)? The Private Nile peaks at Thebes after building through slow river days. Decide the arc so the day-by-day has rhythm — not seven days of equal weight, but a story with a climax. If every day reads the same, the arc is missing.

## The section inventory

**Core** sections appear on every journey page in this fixed order. The day-by-day is the heart; everything else serves it.

| Section | Job | The rule that keeps it good |
|---|---|---|
| **Hero** | Name + the essence of the experience | The lede sells the *feeling*, not the route. "Seven days down the river under sail, at the pace of the wind — and yours." Never a dry route summary. The route goes in the eyebrow. |
| **At-a-glance ledger** | Four facts, scannable | Duration · Route · Style · Pace. Same four, same order, every journey — and matching the destination ledger's pattern so the site feels of-a-piece. |
| **The journey** (overview) | The essence, two paragraphs | Para one: what makes this journey *different in kind* (the convoy vs. the dahabiya). Para two: how the difference plays out in practice. Open with the contrast that justifies the price. |
| **Why it differs** | The access hooks, as a set | 3–4 differentiators, each a hook from decision 3. Numbered (I–IV) — here numbering is *earned*, because these are the discrete reasons-to-buy, not decoration. Each is a thing a convoy genuinely cannot give. |
| **Day by day** (signature) | The itinerary, as the spine | This is the signature module. It is not a flat accordion — it's the spine from decision 1 made visible (see *The spine as layout* below). Every day entry sells the **night/mooring** as well as the day. Operator detail in every day: the *hour*, not just the place. |
| **Before you book** | The honest caveat | A bordered panel. Names who the journey *isn't* for, plainly (decision 2). This is non-negotiable and brand-defining. A journey page without it reads like a brochure and converts worse, not better. |
| **What's included / not included** | Two honest columns | Always both columns. "Included" builds value; "Not included" (flights, visa, gratuities, insurance) pre-empts the awkward proposal surprise and reinforces the advisory tone. Never show only the included side. |
| **Glimpses** | 3 frames, the journey's texture | Fewer than a destination gallery — a journey is shown in moments, not a survey. Caption each with the moment: "Kom Ombo at dusk," "a sandbank mooring." |
| **Your guide** | The human who designed it | Tie the expert *to this itinerary* — "designed this journey mooring by mooring." Not a generic bio; the named person is part of the product and the reason to trust the plan. |
| **Related journeys** | The cross-sell | 3 cards — ideally one shorter, one adjacent, one grander, so the buyer can trade up or sideways. This is the bridge if *this* journey isn't quite the fit. |
| **Final CTA** | "Sail / See [X], properly." | The brand sign-off, plus the response promise ("we reply within 24 hours") and "no obligation." The CTA on a journey page is a *conversation* invitation, not a buy button — these are bespoke proposals. |

### Conditional — only when earned

| Section | Use it when… |
|---|---|
| **The vessel / The dahabiya** | the journey's defining asset deserves its own moment (a Nile sail). Link to a dedicated page if one exists. |
| **A map** | the route's geography genuinely aids understanding (long multi-region journeys like The Complete Egypt). Skip for short, legible routes. |
| **Pricing guidance** | the tier convention allows a "from" figure. If the brand keeps price to the proposal stage, omit entirely — but decide this once, portfolio-wide, not per page. |

## The spine as layout: the day-by-day signature

The day-by-day is where a journey page earns or loses its distinctiveness. A plain stacked accordion is the generic default. Instead, make the **spine visible** — render the structural truth of the journey as the layout itself.

> Does the journey's **way of moving** map onto a **vertical structure** the reader travels down the page?

- **The Private Nile → "Downstream."** A gold river-line runs down the page; each day is a mooring point on it; the days flow from Aswan (top) to Luxor (bottom) as the current does. The layout *is* the journey. (It also reuses the gold-line motif from the destination pages' "Two Banks" — deliberate brand continuity.)
- **The Complete Egypt → a north–south descent.** The spine is geography; the layout could descend from Cairo through Luxor to Abu Simbel, the map narrowing south.
- **A city-pair → contrast.** Two-tone day blocks (the chaos of Cairo / the stillness of the river) could carry the spine.
- **Counter-example.** Do *not* invent a river-line for a journey that doesn't sail, or a descent for one that loops. If the spine is genuinely just "a sequence of good days," a clean numbered timeline is honest and fine. A forced signature is worse than none — the same rule as destination pages.

Whatever the layout: **every day must sell its night.** On a private journey the moorings, the dinners, the where-you-sleep *are* the product. An itinerary that only lists daytime sites is selling half the journey.

## The honesty layer (what separates this brand)

Journey pages live or die on trust, because the buyer is committing days and real money to a plan they can't inspect. Three honesty moves are mandatory on every journey page:

1. **"Before you book."** Who it isn't for, plainly. (Decision 2.)
2. **"Not included."** The real exclusions, stated up front, not discovered at proposal.
3. **Operator judgment in the itinerary.** At least one line per journey that costs you something to say — "the Esna lock is the one stretch every boat must share," "some afternoons are long and unhurried." Naming the journey's honest limits is the strongest possible signal that the rest is true.

A journey page that only sells, never qualifies, reads as a brochure — and at this tier, brochure reads as *cheap*.

## The factual-verification step (journey-specific traps)

Run the shared verification discipline, plus these traps unique to itineraries:

- **Vessel vocabulary.** *Dahabiya*, not felucca, for a multi-night crewed sail — and never claim a chef or cabins on a felucca, which has neither. This is the trap that already appeared twice; guard it on every Nile journey.
- **Direction and geography.** The Nile flows north; Aswan is *south* of Luxor; a downstream sail goes Aswan→Luxor. Getting compass direction wrong in front of a knowledgeable client is fatal to trust.
- **Sequence feasibility.** Verify the day-by-day is physically possible — sailing distances, the Esna lock, site opening days, internal flight timings. A beautiful itinerary that can't actually be sailed in the stated days is a refund.
- **Access claims, dated to days.** Only promise dusk-at-Kom-Ombo or dawn-at-Edfu if it can actually be arranged on that day. Honest hedging ("where available") beats a guarantee you can't keep.
- **Inclusions accuracy.** What's genuinely included vs. billed-at-cost vs. excluded must be exact — this is the line the proposal and the invoice are built on.

> Keep a per-journey *facts ledger* (route, distances, moorings, inclusions, access windows). It's what keeps the EN/ES/JA versions identical and the proposal stage fast.

## Journey ↔ destination linking discipline

The two page types are a system, not silos:

- Every journey page **links to the destination pages** it passes through (Luxor, Aswan), and every destination page's "Related journeys" **links back** to the journeys that include it.
- Keep the facts consistent across both. If the Luxor destination page says the Valley of the Kings is best at opening, the Private Nile's Day 6 must agree. The facts ledgers should reconcile.
- Don't duplicate depth. The destination page carries the *place* in detail; the journey page references it lightly and links. A journey day on Luxor is two sentences and a link, not a second Luxor essay.

## The build order (one session per journey)

1. **Decide the four** (spine, who-it's-for/isn't, hooks-by-day, emotional arc). Write them at the top of the working file. Do not skip to the itinerary.
2. **Draft the day-by-day first.** It's the heart; build the page around it. Each day: the site *and its hour*, plus the night/mooring.
3. **Run factual verification.** Vessel, direction, feasibility, inclusions. Build the journey facts ledger.
4. **Write the framing sections** — overview, why-it-differs, before-you-book, included/not-included — around the verified itinerary.
5. **Choose the spine layout** — does the day-by-day earn a signature, or a clean timeline? Apply the test.
6. **Assemble** from locked components.
7. **The Chanel pass** — remove one thing. Usually an over-sold day or a second superlative.
8. **Trilingual pass** — EN → ES/JA, re-authored, ledger-consistent.

---

## The one-line quality test

> **Would a knowledgeable traveller finish this page trusting that the people who wrote it have actually sailed this river — and trust them enough to hand over seven days and the cost of them?**

If yes, ship it. If it reads like an itinerary assembled to fill seven days rather than designed by someone who knows the hour the light is best at Edfu, it isn't ready — however good it looks.
